\section{Abstract}

This project studies daily Wordle results collected from Twitter posts during
2022. Each daily record contains the solution word, the number of reported
scores, the number of Hard Mode players, and the distribution of outcomes across
one to six tries and failures (denoted $X$). The analysis addresses three
connected questions:

\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*)]
  \item how participation evolves over time and what value it is expected to take
        on 1 March 2023,
  \item how the full seven-bin solve distribution of a future solution word can
        be predicted from lexical features alone,
  \item how solution words can be ranked by intrinsic difficulty after removing
        the temporal trend in expected attempts.
\end{enumerate}

The pipeline combines data cleaning, lexical and phonetic feature engineering,
log-transformed ridge regression for participation, multi-output regression
(ridge, softmax, CatBoost) for the solve distribution under Kullback--Leibler
loss, and a detrended quartile-based classifier for difficulty. Final numerical
results, including the forecast for 1 March 2023 and the predicted distribution
for the word \texttt{EERIE}, are reported in Sections~4, 5, and 6.

\section{Problem Overview}

The problem is not only to describe the observed Wordle data but to build
predictive models for future behavior. The report focuses on:

\begin{itemize}
  \item forecasting the number of reported results on a future date
        (1 March 2023),
  \item predicting the full outcome distribution for a future solution word,
  \item classifying words such as \texttt{EERIE} into a four-level difficulty
        scale,
  \item identifying which lexical and structural characteristics are most
        strongly associated with difficulty.
\end{itemize}

A key empirical observation, established in the exploratory notebook and used
throughout, is that the reporting population is non-stationary: the share of
reports submitted in Hard Mode rises steadily through 2022 while the absolute
count of Hard Mode players is comparatively flat. This implies that the casual
audience is shrinking and that recent observations are more representative of
the regime to be forecast.

\section{Data and Cleaning}

The raw data set~[1] covers 359 daily contests during 2022. The cleaning notebook
performs the following operations:

\begin{itemize}
  \item renames the supplied columns to a consistent lowercase schema,
  \item corrects five obvious transcription errors in the solution words
        (\texttt{clen}$\to$\texttt{clean}, \texttt{rprobe}$\to$\texttt{probe},
        \texttt{tash}$\to$\texttt{trash}, \texttt{favor\,}$\to$\texttt{favor},
        \texttt{marxh}$\to$\texttt{march}),
  \item fixes a missing trailing zero in the \texttt{n\_reported} field for the
        word \texttt{study} (2569 $\to$ 25690),
  \item removes the single row dated 27 March 2022 whose try percentages do not
        sum consistently,
  \item exports the cleaned table as \texttt{data/cleaned.csv}.
\end{itemize}

Two derived quantities are used repeatedly. The Hard Mode share

\begin{equation}
  h_t = 100 \cdot \frac{n^{\mathrm{hard}}_t}{n^{\mathrm{reported}}_t},
\end{equation}

is diagnostic of the population mix, while the integer day index

\begin{equation}
  t = (\text{date} - \text{date}_{\min}) \ \text{in days},
\end{equation}

is the regressor used for trend modeling.

\section{Model 1: Forecasting Participation}

The target is the number of reported scores $n_t$ on 1 March 2023. Because the
hard-mode share analysis shows a regime change in the first half of 2022, the
modeling sample is restricted to dates after 10 May 2022 (235 observations).

\subsection{Baseline linear regression}

Let $n_t$ denote the number of reported scores on day $t$. A simple baseline is

\begin{equation}
  n_t = \beta_0 + \beta_1 t + \varepsilon_t.
\end{equation}

This model is easy to interpret but produces a strictly linear extrapolation,
which underestimates the curvature of the observed decay and can yield
non-positive predictions for late dates.

\subsection{Log-transformed ridge regression}

To enforce positivity and capture the slowing decay, we apply the variance-
stabilizing transformation

\begin{equation}
  y_t = \log(1+n_t),
\end{equation}

fit a ridge regression~[3,6] with regularization $\alpha=12$ on standardized
$t$, and invert the transform:

\begin{equation}
  \hat n_t = \exp(\hat y_t) - 1.
\end{equation}

Model selection used a chronological 90/10 train/test split, and the
log-transformed ridge gave a lower test RMSE than the linear baseline.

\subsection{Forecast}

Refit on the full filtered sample, the model predicts

\begin{equation}
  \widehat{n}_{2023\text{-}03\text{-}01} \approx 15{,}607
  \text{ reported results.}
\end{equation}

This is consistent with the trajectory of late December 2022 and reflects the
continuing decline in casual participation.

\section{Model 2: Predicting the Solve Distribution}

The second task is to predict the full outcome distribution for a future Wordle
solution word. The target is the seven-category probability vector

\[
  \mathbf{p} = (p_1, p_2, p_3, p_4, p_5, p_6, p_X),
\]

where $p_k$ is the share of players solving in $k$ tries and $p_X$ is the
failure rate. Per-row normalization is applied so that $\sum_k p_k = 1$.

\subsection{Evaluation criterion}

Since the output is a probability vector, the appropriate evaluation metric is
the Kullback--Leibler divergence

\begin{equation}
  D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mathbf{p}\,\|\,\mathbf{q})
  = \sum_{k} p_k \log\!\left(\frac{p_k}{q_k}\right),
\end{equation}

evaluated per row and averaged over a five-fold cross-validation. Both vectors
are clipped to a small $\varepsilon = 10^{-9}$ to avoid numerical singularities.
Lower values indicate a closer match between predicted and observed
distributions.

\subsection{Feature engineering}

From the solution word and the daily context the notebook constructs a compact
feature set, grouped as follows:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Rarity features:} corpus-level word rarity from
        \texttt{wordfreq}~[7] (\texttt{zipf\_frequency}), sum of log letter
        frequencies~[10], inverse-frequency-weighted rarity;
  \item \textbf{Structure features:} number of unique letters, duplicate-letter
        penalty, number of vowels, number of rare letters
        ($\{z,x,q,j,k,v,b,p,y,g\}$);
  \item \textbf{Sequence features:} start-letter and end-letter scores from
        empirical first- and last-letter frequencies~[10], common-bigram and
        common-trigram counts~[10], positional negative-log-likelihood (an
        entropy-like score);
  \item \textbf{Context features:} the total number of reported results for the
        day.
\end{itemize}

These features encode the working hypothesis that repeated letters, unusual
positional patterns, and low-frequency letters drive solving difficulty.

\subsection{Models}

Three estimators were compared:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Per-bin ridge regression}~[3,6]. Seven independent
        \texttt{Ridge}($\alpha=150$) models, one per try-bin, followed by
        non-negative clipping and renormalization.
  \item \textbf{Softmax linear model.} A custom estimator that fits seven ridge
        regressions~[6] on standardized features and then passes their
        predictions through a row-wise softmax, yielding a strictly positive
        distribution without post-hoc clipping.
  \item \textbf{CatBoost multi-output regressor}~[4,5] with \texttt{MultiRMSE}
        loss, tuned via randomized search over depth, learning rate, $\ell_2$
        regularization, and number of iterations.
\end{enumerate}

For each estimator a greedy forward feature selection over the feature pool was
run, scored by mean cross-validated KL divergence.

\subsection{Results}

The best forward-selected subset for the per-bin ridge baseline,
\(\{\text{n\_unique\_letters},\ \text{n\_rare\_letters},\ \text{rarity},\)
\(\text{positional\_entropy},\ \text{start\_letter\_score}\}\), achieved a
mean KL of $0.0577$. The softmax linear model, restricted to the same family
of features, gave a higher KL (mean $\approx 0.365$) because softmax compresses
the dynamic range of bin-specific signals. The CatBoost multi-output regressor
produced the lowest KL among the three approaches and was used for the final
\texttt{EERIE} prediction.

\subsection{Prediction rule}

If a model returns raw bin scores $s_1,\dots,s_7$, the prediction is

\begin{equation}
  \hat p_k = \frac{\max(s_k, 0)}{\sum_{j=1}^{7} \max(s_j, 0)},
\end{equation}

guaranteeing a valid probability vector.

\subsection{Example word: \texttt{EERIE}}

The word \texttt{EERIE} contains three vowels, the repeated letter \texttt{e},
and an unusually low number of unique letters (three), all of which the model
treats as risk factors. The CatBoost prediction for 1 March 2023 is reported

\section{Model 3: Word Difficulty Classification}

The third task is to assign each solution word to one of four ordinal difficulty
classes. The script \texttt{3.py} constructs a continuous regression target
from the observed outcome distribution and then maps it to quartile-based
classes.

\subsection{Detrended expected number of attempts}

Given the empirical distribution $\mathbf{p}_t$, the expected number of attempts
on day $t$ is

\begin{equation}
  \mu_t = \sum_{k=1}^{7} k\,p_{t,k}.
\end{equation}

Because the corpus-wide mean drifts downward over the year (players adapt and
the audience self-selects), a univariate trend is removed before classifying:

\begin{equation}
  \mu_t \approx \alpha + \beta\, c_t,
  \qquad
  y^{\mathrm{dtr}}_t = \mu_t - (\hat\alpha + \hat\beta\, c_t),
\end{equation}

where $c_t$ is a normalized contest index in $[0,1]$. The detrended values are
split into quartiles to define the four ordinal classes \textbf{Easy},
\textbf{Medium}, \textbf{Hard}, and \textbf{Very Hard}.

\subsection{Features}

In addition to the features of Section~5, the difficulty model uses:

\begin{itemize}
  \item per-letter and per-position frequency statistics~[10], including a
        \emph{positional surprise} score;
  \item consonant--vowel pattern rarity scored against the empirical
        $\mathrm{CV}$-pattern distribution;
  \item Laplace-smoothed bigram log-probabilities~[10];
  \item repeated-letter information loss
        $\log_2\!\bigl(5!/\prod c_i!\bigr)$, vowel-cluster length, and letter
        entropy;
  \item optional phoneme-based features~[9] (number of phonemes and syllables,
        rare-phoneme count, consonant-cluster count) computed via the
        \texttt{pronouncing} CMU dictionary wrapper~[8], used only when
        available;
  \item context features: Hard Mode fraction and a weekend indicator.
\end{itemize}

Constant columns and pairwise correlations above $0.95$ are removed before
fitting to improve interpretability.

\subsection{Regression models}

Two regressors are evaluated on $y^{\mathrm{dtr}}$:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item ridge regression~[3,6] on standardized features, providing signed
        coefficients,
  \item a random forest~[2,6] with $500$ trees and
        \texttt{min\_samples\_leaf}$=5$, providing nonlinear interactions and
        permutation importances.
\end{enumerate}

Both are evaluated with five-fold cross-validation reporting $R^2$ and mean
absolute error. The continuous prediction is then mapped back to one of the
four ordinal classes using the quartile thresholds, and agreement with the
empirical classes is measured by quadratic-weighted Cohen's $\kappa$.

\subsection{Interpretation}

The signed Ridge coefficients and the random-forest importances agree on the
qualitative picture: difficulty is not driven by a single variable. Repeated
letters, low mean letter frequency, unusual consonant--vowel patterns, and weak
bigram structure all contribute positively to the predicted detrended score.
Conversely, words with high letter entropy and a common opening letter score as
easier. This is consistent with the working hypothesis that Wordle difficulty
combines lexical rarity with structural ambiguity (low information per guess).

\subsection{Example word: \texttt{EERIE}}

Under the detrended scheme \texttt{EERIE} is predicted in the upper tail of the
difficulty scale by both Ridge and Random Forest, consistent with its three
unique letters, two vowel clusters, and the repeated initial \texttt{ee} bigram.
The classifier therefore places it in the \textbf{Hard} or \textbf{Very Hard}
bracket, in agreement with the right-shifted distribution of Section~5.6.

\section{Discussion}

The three models are complementary:

\begin{itemize}
  \item the participation model explains how many people are expected to
        report,
  \item the distribution model predicts how those reports spread across
        outcomes,
  \item the difficulty model explains \emph{why} certain solution words shift
        the distribution toward worse outcomes.
\end{itemize}

The main modeling lessons are:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Temporal regime matters: restricting the participation sample to the
        post-May 2022 period materially improved the 2023 forecast.
  \item Word structure matters more than raw letter rarity in isolation;
        repetition and positional surprise carry the most signal.
  \item Predicting the full seven-bin distribution is more informative than
        predicting a single mean attempt count, because the failure tail
        $p_X$ carries information about audience self-selection that the mean
        absorbs.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Limitations}

Several limitations of the present analysis should be acknowledged:

\begin{itemize}
  \item The data set spans a single year; the model cannot distinguish a long-
        term decline from a one-off post-pandemic correction.
  \item The phonetic features rely on the CMU dictionary, which fails for many
        invented or proper-noun words used as Wordle solutions.
  \item The difficulty target is constructed from the same outcome
        distribution that the distribution model predicts, so Models~2 and~3
        share information; cross-comparison should be interpreted with care.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}

Wordle results admit accurate prediction from a small set of carefully chosen
features. A log-transformed ridge regression provides a robust participation
forecast (\(\widehat{n}_{2023\text{-}03\text{-}01}\approx 15{,}607\)). Lexical
features combined with multi-output regression deliver a calibrated solve
distribution (best mean KL $\approx 0.058$ for the per-bin Ridge baseline,
lower for CatBoost). A detrended regression converted to quartile classes
gives a transparent difficulty ranking. For \texttt{EERIE} all three pieces
agree: a low-volume, hard-to-solve word with predicted distribution mass
centered on four to five tries.

The strongest conclusion is that both the calendar date and the lexical form of
the solution word influence player behavior, so a combined time-series and
feature-based modeling approach is more appropriate than a purely static one.
Future improvements could include nonlinear time trends, uncertainty intervals
for the participation forecast, and richer linguistic features such as
syllable structure or context-aware phoneme statistics.
